# Stories — Business Owner Short Films

**Finished length:** 30–45s · **Built from:** 4–8s shots, plus 15s where a
performance holds · **Assembled in:** Premiere

Adds to `comedy-slate.md` — doesn't replace it. Those ten bits are the comedy
vocabulary; several slot directly into the stories below as beats. A gag is a
moment. A story is a vehicle. We need both, and the gags work harder inside a
story than they ever did alone.

## What makes these stories and not bits

Every one has a person who **wants** something, something **in the way**, and a
**turn** that costs them. The laugh comes from character, not from a punchline
bolted onto the end. And every one lands somewhere honest — you laugh twice,
then something catches you.

Earned cheesy is the target. Cheesy that hasn't been earned is just corny;
cheesy after you've watched someone struggle is the good stuff.

## 01 — THE RIVAL
**~45s · funny in the middle, warm at the end · uses bits 05 and 07**

**Logline:** A barber of thirty years tries to beat the flashy new shop at its
own game, fails badly, and wins by telling the truth instead.

| Beat | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:04 | **Marco**, 60s, sweeps his barbershop. Same floor he's swept for thirty years. Quiet, ordinary, a little worn. |
| 0:04–0:09 | Across the street: a new shop opens. Neon. A DJ. Gorgeous video on a screen in the window. A line down the block. Marco watches through his own glass. |
| 0:09–0:13 | His chairs are empty. He straightens a comb that doesn't need straightening. |
| 0:13–0:22 | **The attempt.** Marco decides to compete. He films himself. It is *rough* — he props the phone on a shampoo bottle, it falls over. He does an awkward thumbs up. He says "we have… good prices" to a camera that's pointed at the ceiling. He watches it back. He deletes it. |
| 0:22–0:30 | **The turn.** An old man in his chair, the only customer of the day. Marco apologizes for the quiet. The old man says, without looking up: *"Marco. You cut my father's hair."* Marco stops moving. |
| 0:30–0:38 | The new video. Not slick. Just Marco, talking, in his own chair — about thirty years, about the man's father, about the floor. It's plain and it's true. |
| 0:38–0:45 | The line is back, out Marco's door. Final shot: the rival barber, from across the street, sitting in Marco's chair getting a cut. They don't speak. Marco is smiling. |

**Why it works:** the failed attempt is the funny part and the sincere attempt is
the payoff, so the comedy and the heart come from the same place. And the rival
in the chair is a better ending than beating him would be.

## 02 — LOOKS DRY
**~45s · the best one · builds bit 06 into a full arc · 9:16 UGC-native**

**Logline:** A baker is wrecked by a single rude comment, and turns it into the
best thing that ever happened to her shop.

| Beat | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:05 | **Renée** pulls croissants at 4am. Flour on her forearms. She's good at this and she knows it. |
| 0:05–0:10 | She posts a photo. Genuinely proud of it. Watches the likes tick. |
| 0:10–0:15 | A comment: **"looks dry."** Username: Deborah. |
| 0:15–0:24 | **The spiral.** She reads it again. She zooms in on her own croissant. She asks her teenage son, who is not equipped for this: "Does it look dry to you?" He says "kinda?" She says "*kinda?!*" She's in her car doing full storytime energy: "It's a *croissant*, Deborah. They're **supposed** to be flaky." |
| 0:24–0:30 | **The low point.** 4am again. She's not baking. She's sitting on an upturned crate looking at her phone. |
| 0:30–0:38 | **The turn.** She goes very still. Then she starts laughing. Cut: a hand-painted sign goes up in the window — **LOOKS DRY.** It's stamped on the boxes. It's on the apron. She's leaned all the way in. |
| 0:38–0:45 | Line out the door, everyone holding a LOOKS DRY box. Then — Deborah walks in. Sheepish. Renée recognizes her instantly. Long beat. *"It's on the house."* |

**Why it works:** a villain, a real wound, a reversal, and grace at the end. It's
funny the whole way and the ending is genuinely lovely. This is the one people
send to each other.

## 03 — THE COMMERCIAL
**~45s · funniest of the set · absorbs bits 01 and 09**

**Logline:** A man makes the worst commercial ever made, and it works — because
it's unmistakably him.

| Beat | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:04 | **Dale**, hardware store, announces to his wife across the kitchen table: "I'm making a commercial." She does not look up from the crossword. "Mm." |
| 0:04–0:20 | **The escalation.** Day one: he writes the jingle himself, 11pm, two-finger typing, thrilled with it. Day two: he rents a mascot costume — it is a hammer, the eyes are wrong, it's genuinely upsetting. Day three: his nephew is "directing," which means holding the phone vertically while eating chips. Day four: Dale is inside the hammer costume in the parking lot in the rain. His wife holds an umbrella over the camera, not over Dale. |
| 0:20–0:26 | **The low point.** They watch the finished thing on the TV. Total silence. His wife, carefully: "It's… you." Dale, quietly: "Yeah." |
| 0:26–0:32 | It airs. Dale watches from behind his hands. |
| 0:32–0:40 | **The turn.** The store phone rings. Then again. A kid comes in and asks Dale to do the jingle. He does the jingle. A small crowd films it. |
| 0:40–0:45 | Final shot: the hammer costume, framed and mounted on the wall behind the register, like a trophy. Dale rings up a customer beneath it, entirely unbothered. |

**Why it works:** pure escalation comedy with a warm landing, and the joke is
never that Dale is stupid — it's that he's *committed*. Commitment is lovable.

## 04 — THE LAST TABLE
**~40s · the emotional one · quietest, most likely to make someone tear up**

**Logline:** A woman sets every table in an empty restaurant, every night, for
people who never come — until someone finally shows the world why.

| Beat | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:06 | Evening. **Ana** sets a table. Then another. Then another. Folds each napkin the same careful way. Fourteen tables. |
| 0:06–0:12 | Pull wide: the dining room is completely empty. It stays empty. She stands at the pass in her whites, waiting. |
| 0:12–0:18 | Her son, closing up: "Ma. You don't have to set them all." She keeps setting them. "They might come." |
| 0:18–0:24 | **Why nobody comes.** Outside: the sign's bulb is out. Her page: one photo, four years old, nothing since. The food is extraordinary and nobody knows the place exists. |
| 0:24–0:32 | **The turn.** Her son starts filming her — not the food. *Her.* Folding napkins for people who haven't arrived. Tasting a sauce at 5pm with nobody in the room. Setting the fourteenth table. |
| 0:32–0:40 | All fourteen tables full. Noise, plates, people. And Ana, moving through it, stops at one table to adjust a fork that was already straight. |

**Why it works:** almost no jokes, and it doesn't need them. Every owner who has
had an empty night will feel this in the chest. Put this one third in a reel,
after two funny ones — the contrast is what makes it hit.

## 05 — THE HANDOFF
**~45s · absorbs bit 03 · father-daughter, generational**

**Logline:** A daughter takes over her father's shop and wants to change
everything; he fights her on all of it, until she changes it without erasing him.

| Beat | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:05 | **Sal** hands over the keys to his daughter **Nina**. He's proud. He's also not letting go of the keys for a beat too long. |
| 0:05–0:14 | Nina starts fixing things. Sal objects to every single one. The register from 1994 — "it works." The sign — "it works." The wood paneling — "your mother liked it." He follows her around the shop defending objects. |
| 0:14–0:22 | **The fight.** She's had enough: "Dad, nobody under fifty knows we exist." Sal, hurt in a way he can't say: "They know *me.*" He leaves. She sits alone in the shop she now owns and doesn't want to break. |
| 0:22–0:32 | **The turn.** She doesn't throw the old stuff out. She builds everything *around* it. The 1994 sign is cleaned, lit, and made the centerpiece. The story on the wall is his story. The photos are of him. |
| 0:32–0:40 | Sal comes in. Stops. Looks at the sign for a long time. Doesn't say anything for a while. Then, quietly: *"That's your mother's handwriting."* |
| 0:40–0:45 | Nina: "I know." Two shot, both looking up at it. |

**Why it works:** the conflict is real and both sides are right, which is what
makes it a story instead of a lesson. The last line does all the work.

## 06 — THE BEDSHEET
**~35s · absorbs bit 02, given an arc**

**Logline:** A guy is convinced his phone photos are good enough, right up until
he sees what his food actually looks like.

| Beat | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:06 | Gorgeous food cinematography. Butter, steam, golden light, orchestral swell. Michelin-grade. |
| 0:06–0:11 | Pull back, and back: a garage, a muffin, a wrinkled bedsheet, a desk lamp with a paper towel taped over it. **Ray**, tongue out in concentration, two hands on the phone. He nods. "That's the one." |
| 0:11–0:18 | He posts it. Nothing happens. He refreshes. Still nothing. He shows it to his wife with real hope — she does the face you do when you love someone. "It's good, babe." |
| 0:18–0:24 | **The low point.** Ray in the empty bakery at closing, looking at the tray of things he made that nobody came for. |
| 0:24–0:32 | **The turn.** Someone shoots it properly. Same muffin. Same recipe. Ray watches the shot come up on the screen and his face changes — he's not impressed, he's a bit emotional. It finally looks like what it tastes like. |
| 0:32–0:35 | He whispers, to nobody: *"That's the one."* Same line. Different everything. |

**Why it works:** the callback carries the entire arc, and the gag from the
opening becomes the emotional payoff. That's the difference between a bit and a
story, in one line.

## The reel order

If these get cut together as a showreel, sequence matters more than picking
favorites:

1. **03 THE COMMERCIAL** — open funniest, buy attention
2. **02 LOOKS DRY** — the shareable one, keep the energy up
3. **04 THE LAST TABLE** — drop into quiet, this is where they feel it
4. **01 THE RIVAL** — back up, funny then warm
5. **05 THE HANDOFF** — close on the generational one

## Production notes

Same discipline as the comedy slate: **lock every character as a still before
generating any video.** These are worse than the bits in that respect — Marco
appears in nine shots and has to be the same man in all nine. Generate one
reference still per character, feed it into every shot.

Story 02 and 04 both have a **low point beat that must be quiet.** Resist filling
it with music or a joke. The silence is what makes the turn land.

Story 06 runs on a **verbatim callback** — "That's the one" at 0:11 and again at
0:32. Same delivery, different weight. Record both readings at once so they
match.
